# Institutional Design — Open Questions and Research Agenda

**Status:** Exploratory; no institutional structure selected  
**Established:** 26 November 2026  
**Related:** [Founding Principles](../FOUNDING_PRINCIPLES.md), [Research Program — Workstream F](../research/PROGRAM.md)

## Purpose

Common Knowledge has a meaningful statement of institutional intent, but it does not yet have a complete institutional design. This document separates commitments already expressed in the founding principles from questions that remain open.

It is not a constitution, legal opinion, governance proposal, or authorization to incorporate.

## 1. Current direction already expressed

The founding principles establish the following intended characteristics:

- Public-benefit purpose and broad access to knowledge.
- Political independence and freedom of inquiry.
- Evidence-led work and correction.
- Openness by default, subject to rights-based protections.
- Resistance to founder, donor, corporate, state, and factional capture.
- Fair compensation without excessive private enrichment.
- Contributor agency, attribution, and respect for labor.
- Transparency, accountability, and meaningful appeal.
- Interoperability, resilience, and long-term preservation.
- A mission and stewardship model that can outlive the founders.
- Responsible human–AI collaboration, with clear human accountability.

These are guiding principles, not yet enforceable institutional safeguards.

## 2. Questions not yet decided

### Legal form and jurisdiction
- What jurisdictions are realistic for initial operations and future international participation?
- Which legal forms can credibly lock assets and purpose to public benefit?
- Could a nonprofit, cooperative, charitable entity, foundation, trust, or hybrid structure best serve the mission?
- What are the tax, reporting, employment, fundraising, liability, and cross-border consequences?
- What professional legal advice is required before accepting funds, employing people, or holding assets?

### Governance and representation
- Who should be members, trustees, directors, stewards, or other accountable decision-makers?
- How should researchers, contributors, users, affected communities, and the public be represented?
- Which decisions are delegated, elected, appointed, reserved, or appealable?
- How are conflicts of interest disclosed and managed?
- What checks prevent founder, board, donor, government, corporate, or factional capture?
- How can governance remain accessible without becoming vulnerable to manipulation or paralysis?

### Mission protection and succession
- Which purposes and constraints should be legally or structurally protected?
- How can mission changes be proposed, reviewed, approved, and challenged?
- How can leadership be replaced and institutional knowledge transferred?
- What happens if the organization becomes insolvent, inactive, captured, or unable to maintain its infrastructure?
- How should assets and records be transferred if the institution dissolves?

### Economics and labor
- What constitutes fair and sustainable compensation across roles and regions?
- What limits or review mechanisms should apply to executive and founder compensation?
- How should surplus funds be allocated among reserves, maintenance, grants, shared infrastructure, and public programs?
- What funding mix reduces dependence on any one donor, state, corporation, or grant program?
- How should volunteer labor, paid labor, contracting, and contributor recognition coexist?

### Ownership, rights, and stewardship
- Who holds trademarks, domains, software copyrights, datasets, documentation, and infrastructure assets?
- How should software, documentation, data, metadata, and third-party works be licensed separately?
- What rights do contributors retain, and what permissions does the institution need?
- How should privacy, consent, Indigenous data sovereignty, community-controlled knowledge, and sensitive research be respected?
- What happens to user data and contributor work when a service closes?

### Research independence and institutional conduct
- How are research priorities set without suppressing unpopular questions?
- How are politically consequential findings communicated without partisan campaigning?
- What protections exist for whistleblowers, critics, and researchers facing pressure?
- How are research integrity concerns, corrections, misconduct allegations, and appeals handled fairly?
- How should funders and partners be prevented from controlling conclusions?

## 3. Design principles to test as safeguards

For each proposed structure, test whether it provides practical mechanisms—not merely promises—for:
- Distributed and accountable authority.
- Transparent finances and conflicts of interest.
- Independent review and accessible appeals.
- Protected freedom of inquiry.
- Limits on private benefit and asset diversion.
- Contributor and community rights.
- Leadership succession and continuity.
- Resilience to funding loss, political pressure, and institutional failure.
- Meaningful participation across geography, language, disability, and institutional status.

## 4. Research method

Workstream F should compare real organizations and legal instruments using primary governing documents wherever possible. For each case, examine:
- Founding documents, bylaws, statutes, and legal form.
- Membership and board selection.
- Reserved powers, vetoes, and amendment thresholds.
- Asset locks, dissolution clauses, and private-benefit rules.
- Funding concentration and disclosure.
- Compensation and labor policies.
- Contributor and data rights.
- Documented disputes, governance failures, and successful safeguards.
- Whether formal protections operated effectively in practice.

Separate what governing documents say from how an organization behaves. Identify jurisdiction-specific questions for qualified legal counsel. Include failed and captured organizations, not only celebrated examples.

## 5. Current non-decisions

No legal entity, jurisdiction, board structure, membership model, asset lock, compensation formula, funding policy, trademark ownership model, or final licensing policy has been selected. The public Git repository currently has no selected project license; do not infer permission to reuse or contribute under a particular license.

The project should not rush incorporation or fundraising merely to appear established. At the same time, legal and governance design must be addressed before the project begins accepting substantial assets, employing people, collecting sensitive data, or representing itself as a durable public institution.

## 6. Next research priorities

1. Map legal forms and mission-lock mechanisms in plausible jurisdictions.
2. Compare governance models and their real-world capture/failure modes.
3. Examine funding concentration, compensation, and asset stewardship.
4. Draft a decision framework and identify questions requiring professional advice.
5. Revisit the design with a broader group of contributors and affected stakeholders before adoption.
