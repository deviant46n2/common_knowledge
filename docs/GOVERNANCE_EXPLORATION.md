# Governance and Community — Working Exploration

**Status:** Exploratory discussion; not policy, constitution, or settled governance design  
**Started:** 26 November 2026  
**Related:** [Founding Principles](../FOUNDING_PRINCIPLES.md) · [Institutional Design — Deferred Questions](INSTITUTIONAL_DESIGN.md) · [Transparency](TRANSPARENCY.md)

This document preserves an ongoing discussion about how Common Knowledge might attract a large contributor community and eventually become democratically governed. It records current instincts, emerging principles, risks, and unresolved questions. It deliberately does not prescribe a final constitution, legal structure, membership system, or voting procedure.

The central constraint is important: detailed governance should be developed with the people who actually participate in and depend on Common Knowledge. Recording these discussions is not a substitute for that future participation.

## 1. The aspiration

The long-term aspiration is that, if Common Knowledge grows to thousands of participants, the institution should ultimately follow the will of its community through legitimate democratic processes.

The institution should belong to the community that sustains it, not to the people who happen to control it at a particular moment.

This implies:
- The founder should not retain permanent or unchecked authority.
- Maintainers should not automatically own the institution's direction.
- Donors and sponsors should not be able to purchase governance power.
- Contributors should have meaningful ways to shape the project, challenge decisions, and replace leaders.
- The institution should be able to change its governance model, but not casually or through a temporary majority acting without deliberation.

This is an aspiration for future stewardship, not a description of current arrangements. Common Knowledge is currently founder-led.

## 2. Emerging model: representation for operations, direct democracy for consequential questions

A working hypothesis is to combine elected or otherwise democratically accountable representatives for everyday institutional administration with direct community participation for especially consequential decisions.

Potential division of responsibility:

- **Routine operations:** Delegated teams, maintainers, and elected representatives operate within clearly defined authority.
- **Major institutional decisions:** The community has direct, meaningful influence through open proposals, deliberation, and votes.
- **Constitutional changes:** A demanding, transparent, multi-stage amendment process, potentially including a cooling-off period and more than one approval stage.
- **Special deliberation:** Randomly selected community assemblies could examine complex or consequential proposals, hear competing arguments, and publish recommendations before a wider community decision.

This is not yet a chosen system. It is a direction worth exploring because it may combine day-to-day practicality with community sovereignty.

## 3. Constitutional durability and amendment

An emerging preference is that governance should be difficult to change, but possible to change—similar to a constitution.

The underlying idea:
- The community should ultimately have authority over the institution.
- Fundamental governance rules should be stable enough to protect the institution from impulsive or opportunistic changes.
- The community should retain a legitimate path to amend those rules as experience and circumstances change.
- Ordinary operational policies should be easier to change than constitutional provisions.

A future amendment process might involve a public proposal, a substantial deliberation period, publication of alternatives and objections, independent procedural review, a demanding approval threshold, and possibly a second confirmation after a cooling-off period.

No thresholds, membership rules, or procedures have been selected.

A further unresolved issue is whether certain protections should be entrenched against removal through the ordinary amendment process—for example, meaningful democratic participation, the right to criticize and challenge authority, and the continued existence of a legitimate amendment process. This requires careful thought; no list of unamendable provisions has been adopted.

## 4. Distinguish contribution, expertise, and governance

A healthy community should not equate writing the most code with deserving the most political power.

These are distinct:
- **Contribution:** Helping improve the project in any meaningful way.
- **Expertise:** Demonstrated knowledge relevant to a particular technical, scientific, or operational question.
- **Governance:** Legitimate authority to participate in decisions about the institution.

Expertise should inform technical and knowledge-related decisions without automatically granting institutional political authority. Contributions in research, documentation, review, translation, accessibility, maintenance, community support, and other areas should be valued alongside engineering.

A future membership model must avoid both unrestricted vulnerability to manipulation and an entrenched insider class. The question of who gets to vote remains open.

## 5. Democracy is not a method for determining facts

Common Knowledge must distinguish institutional governance from knowledge evaluation.

- Democratic processes can decide institutional priorities, budgets, leadership, policies, and the direction of the project.
- Claims about the world should be assessed through evidence, methods, reasoning, uncertainty, and open criticism—not decided by popularity or majority vote.
- Credentials and expertise matter as evidence of relevant competence, but authority alone does not establish that a claim is true.
- Institutional governance must not dictate research conclusions to protect the institution, its funders, or a majority viewpoint.

This distinction is central to the aspiration that Common Knowledge be a **knowledge authority, not a truth authority**.

## 6. Failure modes to design against

Radical democracy and elected representation both have vulnerabilities. The following risks have been identified for future design work.

### 6.1 Majority tyranny
A majority could restrict legitimate inquiry, silence a minority, or remove protections from unpopular groups.

**Possible response to investigate:** Constitutional rights and protections that ordinary votes cannot override; meaningful appeal and dissent mechanisms.

### 6.2 Participation inequality
A small, highly active minority could effectively govern a much larger, less active membership. Time, confidence, language, disability, wealth, and social influence can affect who participates.

**Possible responses to investigate:** Asynchronous participation, accessible processes, reasonable voting windows, transparent participation statistics, and randomly selected deliberative assemblies. Do not assume the most active participants represent everyone.

### 6.3 Popularity replacing informed judgment
Votes could favor fashionable but technically unsound proposals, or suppress unpopular research.

**Possible response to investigate:** Keep institutional decision-making separate from evidence assessment; publish technical analysis and dissent without turning expertise into unchecked political authority.

### 6.4 Manipulation and coordinated capture
Fake identities, coordinated campaigns, harassment, wealthy organizations, or political actors could try to distort participation.

**Possible responses to investigate:** Transparent election administration, proportionate membership rules, conflict disclosures, deliberation periods, auditable procedures, and protections against intimidation. Membership restrictions must not become a means of excluding newcomers or entrenching insiders.

### 6.5 Representative capture
Elected officials could become a self-protecting political class, control information, or make themselves difficult to replace.

**Possible responses to investigate:** Limited terms, public records and explanations, independent election administration, recall or removal mechanisms, and limits on representatives changing the rules governing their own authority.

### 6.6 Decision paralysis
Extensive deliberation for every operational decision could make the institution unable to respond to routine needs or urgent risks.

**Possible responses to investigate:** Clear delegation, defined decision classes, and narrowly scoped emergency powers with expiration, disclosure, and retrospective review.

### 6.7 Manufactured consensus
A vote may appear decisive even when dissenters were intimidated, information was incomplete, or a charismatic participant dominated discussion.

**Possible responses to investigate:** Preserve proposals, alternatives, evidence, conflicts of interest, objections, minority statements, participation data, and reasons for the final decision. A vote count alone is not a sufficient record of democratic legitimacy.

## 7. Transparency and accountability

Two complementary principles have emerged:

**Transparency should make power inspectable; democracy should make power accountable.**

Transparency should extend beyond publishing vote results. For consequential decisions, a public record should aim to preserve:
- The original proposal and its authorship.
- Alternatives considered.
- Arguments for and against.
- Relevant evidence and analysis.
- Conflicts of interest.
- Participation and voting procedures and results.
- Objections and minority views.
- The decision, its rationale, and how it may be reviewed or challenged.

Transparency must still respect privacy, safety, consent, and legitimate security needs. Those limits should be proportionate and should not become a pretext for hiding misconduct or avoiding accountability.

## 8. Contributor culture and scaling

The project should prepare for growth without imposing a large bureaucracy before there is a community that needs it.

Potential progression:
- **Very early community:** Build habits of public discussion, clear contribution paths, helpful reviews, and respectful disagreement. Avoid premature hierarchy.
- **Growing community:** Document areas of responsibility, decision records, and maintainer expectations. Make authority visible and transferable.
- **Larger community:** Develop working groups, transparent selection and removal processes, appeals, and clear decision boundaries.
- **Mature institution:** Develop formal democratic governance, independent accountability, and safeguards against capture with the participation of the people affected.

Participation should not require everyone to attend frequent meetings or be an experienced programmer. Newcomers should be able to understand the project's purpose, current work, contribution paths, decision processes, and who holds which responsibilities.

## 9. Questions still open

These are discussion prompts, not decisions awaiting automatic implementation.

1. Who should constitute the voting community: users, contributors, members, or a broader combination?
2. Should different kinds of meaningful participation confer equal voting standing? If so, how should membership be established without creating barriers or an insider class?
3. Which decisions are routine, consequential, or constitutional?
4. Which powers should be delegated to elected representatives, and what limits and accountability should apply?
5. What decisions require direct community approval?
6. Should randomly selected assemblies be advisory, decision-making, or both depending on the question?
7. What approval thresholds, quorum rules, and cooling-off periods are appropriate for constitutional amendments?
8. Which rights, if any, should be protected from ordinary amendment?
9. How can the institution respond quickly to security, safety, or legal emergencies without normalizing executive overreach?
10. How should non-members, affected communities, and people whose knowledge or data is represented in the commons participate in decisions that affect them?
11. How should the institution protect dissent and minority rights while preserving the community's ability to change direction?

## 10. Working commitments for now

Until a real community exists to shape governance, the practical commitments are modest:

- Be honest that the project is currently founder-led.
- Make important development decisions and relevant project finances visible from the beginning.
- Welcome criticism and alternative proposals.
- Do not imply that contributors already possess formal governance rights that have not been established.
- Do not create permanent founder privileges or make irreversible governance, legal, ownership, or funding commitments prematurely.
- Preserve these discussions so future contributors can inspect the origins and evolution of the project's thinking.
- Revisit governance as the community, product, resources, and institutional responsibilities develop.

## 11. Public ownership and stewardship

A clear founder-level conviction has now been stated: **Common Knowledge belongs to everyone.** This is stronger than saying it belongs only to the people who actively build, maintain, fund, or govern it.

The institution's intended beneficiaries are the public as a whole, including people who may never create an account, submit a contribution, attend a meeting, or participate in governance. The people who do the work are stewards of a public-benefit resource, not its owners.

For future design, distinguish three overlapping constituencies:

- **The public:** Everyone for whose benefit the knowledge infrastructure exists. This includes non-users and people who may be affected by the institution's decisions.
- **The community:** People who participate in, use, shape, discuss, or help sustain Common Knowledge. The boundaries and rights of this community remain to be defined.
- **The stewards:** People entrusted with specific responsibilities for building, maintaining, funding, or operating the institution and its infrastructure. Stewardship should be accountable, limited, and transferable—not a claim of ownership.

These categories should overlap, but they should not be collapsed into one another. In particular, doing more work should not automatically confer ownership of the institution or an unlimited claim to govern everyone else's interests.

This conviction sharpens several unresolved governance questions:

1. What does it mean in practical and legal terms for an institution to belong to everyone, rather than to its founder, contributors, members, or funders?
2. How can people who never participate directly still be protected from institutional abuse and have meaningful routes to raise concerns?
3. How should the community's decision-making authority relate to the interests of the wider public?
4. How can stewards receive enough delegated authority to operate the institution effectively while remaining accountable to its public purpose?
5. What safeguards prevent an active contributor group from becoming a permanent insider class or claiming to represent the whole public without legitimate authorization?

**Status:** The public-benefit orientation and the conviction that Common Knowledge belongs to everyone are founder-stated principles. The three-constituency distinction is a working conceptual model, not a settled membership or governance design. Legal ownership, public representation, and accountability mechanisms remain open questions.


## 12. No permanent governance privilege for contributors or founders

A further founder-stated principle clarifies the relationship between contribution and authority:

- Contributors should not receive special political or institutional powers merely because they contribute code, research, money, time, or other work.
- Early contributors may be consulted more closely during the formative stage, particularly on software direction, technical architecture, and the roadmap. This is consultative influence based on relevant experience, not a special vote or governing entitlement.
- Once Common Knowledge is an established platform, even this early-contributor consultation should not become a permanent privileged class. Relevant experience remains worth hearing, but early arrival alone should not confer enduring authority.
- Practical responsibilities may require delegated powers—for example, maintainers need authority to maintain systems within their remit. Such powers are role-specific and accountable, not general political privilege.
- The founder likewise does not seek permanent personal control. The founding ideals should be protected through durable institutional principles and safeguards, not through an eternal founder veto.

This expresses a deliberate tension rather than resolving it: the founder wants the original purpose and ideals protected as strongly as reasonably possible, while recognizing that a genuinely community-governed institution must ultimately trust its community and cannot remain under the founder's control forever.

A useful distinction for future design is:

- **Equal standing:** No special political status based on contribution history, donor status, technical prestige, or founder status.
- **Consultation:** Seek informed advice from people with relevant knowledge and experience, without granting them extra authority.
- **Delegated responsibility:** Give people the limited powers necessary to perform assigned operational roles, with transparent boundaries and accountability.
- **Foundational protections:** Preserve the institution's public purpose and core commitments through mechanisms that do not depend on one person's permanent control.

**Status:** The rejection of permanent contributor privilege and founder privilege is a founder-stated principle. Early-stage consultation of experienced contributors on product and roadmap matters is a working approach. The exact protections for founding ideals—and how they can be amended by a future community—remain unresolved.


## 13. Founding values: durable, visible, and amendable

The founder rejects the idea that their personal judgment should carry greater authority in perpetuity. Future generations should be able to change Common Knowledge's core values through a sufficiently rigorous and legitimate process.

The emerging principle is:

**Core values should be clearly and transparently visible. Changing them should be difficult, but never impossible.**

Implications to explore:

- Publish the core values prominently, with explanations of their meaning and the reasons they were adopted.
- Make the distinction between core values and ordinary policies clear. Values should not be silently reinterpreted or effectively changed through routine policy decisions.
- Require a demanding, transparent process for amending core values, with substantial deliberation and broad democratic agreement.
- Do not give the founder a permanent veto or special interpretive authority over future generations.
- Preserve the history of the original principles and every subsequent amendment, including the proposal, arguments, decision process, outcome, and rationale. Amendment should create an inspectable institutional history, not erase the past.
- Allow future communities to revise even fundamental principles when they reach a legitimate and considered decision to do so.

The goal is continuity without permanent founder control: the institution should remember its origins and take them seriously, while remaining capable of learning and changing.

**Status:** This is a founder-stated principle about the durability and amendability of core values. The values themselves, their exact constitutional status, and the amendment procedure remain to be developed with the community. This is not a finalized constitution or list of entrenched rights.
