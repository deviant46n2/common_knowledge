# Common Knowledge Research Program

**Status:** Active planning document  
**Established:** 26 November 2026  
**Current research edition:** [November 2026 snapshot](snapshots/2026-11/README.md)  
**Method:** [Research methodology](methodology.md)

## Purpose

This program is the durable working plan for understanding the ecosystem that Common Knowledge may contribute to. It exists so the work can continue across chat sessions, contributors, and changes in personnel without depending on anyone's memory of informal conversations.

The immediate goal is not to justify building a particular platform. It is to understand what already exists, how well it serves real people and research needs, where its limitations are, and whether Common Knowledge could make a useful contribution. That contribution might be software, integration, standards work, documentation, community-building, funding, advocacy, or support for existing institutions. Building new infrastructure is only one possible outcome.

The program is a living plan. Workstream boundaries and priorities may change when evidence warrants it. Changes should be documented rather than silently rewriting the history of decisions.

## Research status

At the time this program was created, the seven workstreams below are defined, but they have **not** been comprehensively investigated. A workstream's existence or folder does not imply that its research is complete.

Use these status labels in workstream records:

- **Not started** — scope is recorded, investigation has not begun.
- **In progress** — sources and analysis are being assembled.
- **Needs review** — a draft exists and needs source checking or independent critique.
- **Active / partial** — useful findings exist, but important areas remain unexamined.
- **Baseline complete** — a bounded first-pass report exists, with coverage and limitations stated. This does not mean the field is completely mapped.
- **Superseded** — a later edition or revised approach replaces this work; retain the historical record.

Update the status and a short progress note when meaningful work is completed.

## The seven workstreams

### A. Open science and research collaboration

**Central question:** How do people and institutions currently discover, organize, conduct, review, publish, share, and reuse research collaboratively?

Investigate:
- Open-science platforms, research collaboration environments, project registries, and scholarly infrastructure.
- Citizen science, community science, independent research, participatory research, and public involvement.
- Research lifecycle support: discovery, project coordination, protocols, data, analysis, review, publication, and reuse.
- Peer review, preregistration, research assessment, publication models, and barriers to participation.
- How academic, independent, community, and industry research connect—or fail to connect.
- Who can participate in practice, including barriers involving cost, credentials, geography, language, disability, and institutional affiliation.

Expected outputs:
- A map of relevant platforms, organizations, communities, and practices.
- A lifecycle view of what is well-supported and where people encounter friction.
- A comparison of collaboration and participation models.
- Evidence-backed unmet needs and opportunities.

Workstream brief: [A — Open science and research collaboration](snapshots/2026-11/workstreams/A-open-science/README.md).

### B. Distributed and federated infrastructure

**Central question:** What infrastructure allows research resources, services, data, and computing to be shared across independent participants without requiring one central operator to control everything?

Investigate:
- Volunteer and distributed computing, grid computing, cloud and edge approaches.
- Federated identity, service federation, decentralized coordination, and peer-to-peer systems.
- Distributed storage, replication, mirrors, archival systems, and data movement.
- Work scheduling, task verification, fault tolerance, security, abuse resistance, and heterogeneous hardware.
- Resource accounting, incentives, costs, energy use, and environmental impact.
- Deployment and operational burden for individuals, laboratories, and institutions.
- The difference between technical distribution and meaningful distribution of ownership, governance, and control.

Expected outputs:
- An architecture and project landscape.
- A comparison of trust, coordination, verification, and resilience models.
- A practical account of what can be distributed and what still requires trusted coordination.
- Candidate integration opportunities and carefully scoped technical experiments.

Workstream brief: [B — Distributed and federated infrastructure](snapshots/2026-11/workstreams/B-distributed-infrastructure/README.md).

### C. Scientific reproducibility and research integrity

**Central question:** What makes research inspectable, reproducible, verifiable, correctable, and trustworthy across different people, tools, institutions, and time?

Investigate:
- Reproducible computational environments, workflows, notebooks, containers, and research software.
- Data and code availability, metadata, provenance, versioning, and persistent identifiers.
- Experimental design, preregistration, replication, validation, peer review, and evidence synthesis.
- Research integrity, corrections, retractions, negative results, conflicts of interest, and misconduct handling.
- Reproducibility barriers in different disciplines, including research that cannot or should not release all underlying data.
- How AI-assisted research should record model versions, prompts or procedures where relevant, tool use, human oversight, and generated artifacts.
- Practical differences between repeatability, reproducibility, replicability, and independent verification; use discipline-appropriate definitions rather than assuming one universal vocabulary.

Expected outputs:
- A map of tools, standards, and established practices.
- A research-record and provenance requirements analysis.
- A practical reproducibility checklist or reference workflow.
- Risks and gaps, including cases where openness conflicts with privacy, safety, or consent.

Workstream brief: [C — Reproducibility and research integrity](snapshots/2026-11/workstreams/C-reproducibility/README.md).

### D. Human–AI research collaboration

**Central question:** How can people and AI systems contribute to research in ways that are useful, accountable, inspectable, and appropriately bounded?

Investigate:
- AI support for literature discovery, synthesis, hypothesis generation, coding, analysis, simulation, and writing.
- Multi-agent and multi-model collaboration, task decomposition, coordination, and shared memory.
- Human oversight, expert review, responsibility, attribution, and contributor consent.
- Evaluation of AI contributions, including reliability, calibration, uncertainty, reproducibility, and error detection.
- Model diversity, open models, local inference, hosted services, and resource constraints.
- Data leakage, privacy, security, prompt injection, automation bias, fabricated sources, and other failure modes.
- How to distinguish AI assistance from independent evidence or scientific validation.
- Whether AI participation broadens access or reinforces existing inequalities and concentrations of power.

Expected outputs:
- A landscape of research-oriented AI tools and collaboration approaches.
- An evaluation framework for AI contributions.
- A set of safe, transparent, human-accountable workflow patterns.
- Clearly bounded experiments that test claims rather than merely demonstrate impressive behavior.

Workstream brief: [D — Human–AI research collaboration](snapshots/2026-11/workstreams/D-human-ai-collaboration/README.md).

### E. Shared knowledge and preservation

**Central question:** How can knowledge be represented, connected, attributed, maintained, discovered, and preserved as a durable shared resource?

Investigate:
- Open repositories, libraries, archives, knowledge bases, scholarly indexes, and public datasets.
- Knowledge graphs, ontologies, metadata, taxonomies, semantic standards, and cross-domain linking.
- Citation, attribution, provenance, version history, corrections, and competing interpretations.
- Digital preservation, replication, format migration, institutional continuity, and long-term access.
- Search and discovery across languages, disciplines, formats, and levels of expertise.
- Accessibility, public understanding, education, and pathways from primary evidence to understandable explanations.
- Licensing and rights, including public domain, open licenses, third-party rights, Indigenous data sovereignty, and community-controlled knowledge.
- Appropriate limits on publication: personal data, human-subject research, sensitive ecological or cultural information, and safety-sensitive material.

Expected outputs:
- A map of major knowledge repositories, preservation efforts, and standards.
- An analysis of interoperability and knowledge-linking challenges.
- A proposed model for traceable, versioned knowledge records.
- A rights-aware view of what should be open, restricted, or governed by affected communities.

Workstream brief: [E — Knowledge and preservation](snapshots/2026-11/workstreams/E-knowledge-preservation/README.md).

### F. Governance, economics, and institutional design

**Central question:** What organizational, legal, economic, and governance arrangements can sustain a shared knowledge commons while resisting capture and protecting contributors and the public interest?

Investigate:
- Nonprofits, cooperatives, public institutions, foundations, federations, commons trusts, and hybrid structures.
- Ownership and stewardship of software, data, infrastructure, trademarks, funds, and other assets.
- Decision-making, representation, accountability, dispute resolution, appeals, and leadership succession.
- Funding models: grants, donations, memberships, public funding, service revenue, institutional support, and shared infrastructure.
- Compensation, fair labor, volunteer expectations, conflicts of interest, and limits on private enrichment.
- Risks of donor, state, corporate, founder, or factional capture.
- Political independence, freedom of inquiry, academic freedom, and protections against both partisan weaponization and politically motivated suppression.
- Contributor rights, licensing, liability, privacy obligations, jurisdiction, and long-term continuity.
- What can be learned from failures as well as successful institutions.

Expected outputs:
- A comparative map of institutional and economic models.
- A set of governance failure modes and safeguards.
- Questions requiring qualified legal, financial, or governance advice.
- Options and trade-offs for Common Knowledge, without prematurely selecting a legal structure.

Workstream brief: [F — Governance and economics](snapshots/2026-11/workstreams/F-governance-economics/README.md).

### G. People, needs, and the research ecosystem

**Central question:** What do researchers, students, independent contributors, communities, institutions, and the public actually need—and what prevents them from participating or benefiting?

Investigate:
- Researchers across academic, public, private, independent, and community settings.
- Research support staff, librarians, data stewards, software engineers, educators, funders, and infrastructure operators.
- Citizen scientists, subject-matter volunteers, local and Indigenous communities, and people outside established institutions.
- Practical barriers: time, funding, credentials, equipment, internet access, language, accessibility, trust, legal constraints, and recognition.
- Existing communities, networks, professional associations, and informal collaboration practices.
- Incentives, motivations, burnout, contributor retention, credit, and ownership.
- Unmet needs reported directly by affected people, not inferred solely from platform features or founder assumptions.

Use ethical, proportionate methods for interviews, surveys, and observation. Obtain informed consent where appropriate, minimize collection of personal data, and do not publish identifiable or sensitive information without a valid basis and permission.

Expected outputs:
- A stakeholder and needs map.
- A record of conversations and themes, with consent and privacy respected.
- A comparison of stated needs with existing services and barriers.
- A prioritized set of problems validated by people who experience them.

Workstream brief: [G — People and needs](snapshots/2026-11/workstreams/G-people-and-needs/README.md).

## How the workstreams connect

The workstreams are lenses on one connected ecosystem, not isolated departments.

- A describes the research and collaboration activities people need to carry out.
- B examines the infrastructure and resource-sharing arrangements that may support those activities.
- C examines the quality, traceability, and trustworthiness of research outputs.
- D examines the role of AI systems within those workflows.
- E examines how knowledge is connected, made accessible, and preserved.
- F examines who stewards the commons, how it is funded, and how power is constrained.
- G tests all of the above against the needs and experiences of actual people.

Cross-workstream analysis should identify dependencies and tensions. For example, technical decentralization may conflict with privacy or operational simplicity; openness may conflict with consent or community rights; automation may improve throughput while weakening accountability; and a sustainable funding model may introduce capture risks.

## Recommended sequence

This is a suggested order for building understanding, not a rigid dependency graph.

1. **Stage 1 — Understand the foundations:** begin with A (open science and collaboration), C (reproducibility and integrity), and E (knowledge and preservation). These establish what research needs to accomplish, what makes its outputs trustworthy, and how knowledge remains usable.
2. **Stage 2 — Examine enabling technology:** investigate B (distributed and federated infrastructure) and D (human–AI collaboration), informed by the needs and constraints found in Stage 1.
3. **Stage 3 — Examine stewardship:** investigate F (governance, economics, and institutional design), using real examples and failure cases rather than designing governance in the abstract.
4. **Stage 4 — Validate with people throughout:** G is listed as a stage for consolidating stakeholder research, but conversations with researchers and communities should begin from the start and inform every workstream.
5. **Stage 5 — Synthesize:** compare findings across all seven workstreams, identify genuinely underserved needs, and recommend whether Common Knowledge should build, integrate, partner, fund, document, or do something else.

Do not wait until Stage 4 to speak to people. Do not treat the sequence as a reason to postpone an important question that emerges earlier.

## Standard investigation cycle

Use this cycle for each workstream and major topic:

1. **Define the question.** State what we need to learn, why it matters, and what is outside scope.
2. **Map the landscape.** Find projects, organizations, communities, standards, and relevant literature across multiple search terms and disciplines.
3. **Select cases for investigation.** Explain why each case is relevant; avoid collecting names without understanding them.
4. **Inspect primary evidence.** Read documentation, source code, papers, governance records, policies, releases, and other material appropriate to the claims.
5. **Seek independent evidence.** Look for evaluations, user experience, critical discussion, replication, operational evidence, and known failures.
6. **Record structured profiles.** Use the project, organization, and other relevant templates; attach sources to claims.
7. **Compare approaches.** Identify what is genuinely comparable, what differs, and where evidence is missing.
8. **Identify needs and gaps.** Connect shortcomings to affected users and evidence; do not label something a gap merely because no tool matches our preferred design.
9. **Develop options.** Consider integration, partnership, standards, documentation, community support, funding, or new development.
10. **Review and synthesize.** Record limitations, confidence, disagreements, and what new evidence would change the conclusions.

## Standard deliverables

Each workstream should aim to produce:

- **Field overview:** scope, terminology, history, major patterns, and research questions.
- **Landscape directory:** relevant projects, organizations, communities, standards, and sources, with inclusion rationale.
- **Case profiles:** deeper investigations of selected examples using the relevant templates.
- **Comparative analysis:** approaches, trade-offs, maturity, evidence, and limitations.
- **Synthesis report:** established findings, assessments, unresolved questions, unmet needs, and implications for Common Knowledge.

Not every workstream needs the same number of profiles or identical report structure. Depth should follow relevance and evidence, not a quota.

## Shared comparison dimensions

Use these dimensions where relevant. They are prompts for analysis, **not a universal numerical scorecard**:

- **Openness:** source, data, methods, standards, licensing, and practical access—recorded separately.
- **Decentralization:** distribution of infrastructure, control, ownership, decision-making, and failure risk.
- **Accessibility:** cost, usability, disability access, language, connectivity, credentials, and geographic reach.
- **Reliability and maturity:** demonstrated operation, maintenance, testing, incident history, and support.
- **Reproducibility and verifiability:** traceability, provenance, independent checking, and repeatability where appropriate.
- **Interoperability and portability:** standards, APIs, exportability, migration, and dependence on specific providers.
- **Governance and accountability:** who decides, who can challenge decisions, transparency, and remedies.
- **Sustainability:** funding, staffing, maintenance, energy, and continuity.
- **Contributor rights and agency:** consent, attribution, control, compensation, privacy, and ability to leave or withdraw contributions where applicable.
- **Resilience and preservation:** redundancy, succession, archives, and resistance to censorship or capture.
- **Safety and responsibility:** security, misuse, human rights, environmental effects, and protection of sensitive information.
- **Demonstrated impact:** who uses it, what it enables, and what evidence supports claims of benefit.

Do not collapse these dimensions into a single score. Explain trade-offs and context.

## Progress tracking and continuity

At the end of a meaningful research session:

1. Update the relevant workstream brief with what was investigated.
2. Add or revise project and organization profiles.
3. Add source records and citations close to the supported claims.
4. Update the workstream's status, last-worked date, and next concrete steps.
5. Update cross-workstream synthesis or gaps only when the evidence supports it.
6. Record decisions, uncertainties, and changes to the plan.
7. Commit the documentation so the current state survives beyond the session.

A future contributor should be able to answer from the repository:
- What are we trying to learn?
- What has actually been investigated?
- Which claims have sources?
- What is assessment rather than fact?
- What remains unknown?
- What should happen next?

## Initial priorities

Start with a bounded, useful first pass rather than trying to map the entire world at once.

- Establish definitions and boundaries for the first workstream topics.
- Identify major existing efforts and foundational standards.
- Investigate a small number of representative cases deeply.
- Seek critical and independent evidence, not only project descriptions.
- Start conversations with people who do the work.
- Record the gaps in our own coverage as carefully as gaps in the ecosystem.

The first research question should be selected based on relevance, tractability, and the likelihood of learning something actionable—not because it is the easiest technology to build.

## Decision rule

The research should remain capable of changing our minds. If the evidence shows that existing organizations already meet a need, Common Knowledge should consider supporting or connecting them rather than duplicating their work. If a proposed feature does not correspond to a demonstrated need, defer it. If a promising idea creates unacceptable risks to privacy, freedom of inquiry, contributor agency, or public accountability, redesign or reject it.

The purpose of this program is to help Common Knowledge become useful and trustworthy—not to prove that the original idea was right.
