# Research Methodology

This document defines how the Common Knowledge Research Library is assembled and maintained. The goal is not to create an illusion of certainty; it is to make research traceable, useful, and honest about its limits.

## 1. Research questions and scope

Each research cycle should begin with explicit questions. Examples:

- What already exists to support open, collaborative, reproducible research?
- Which parts of the research lifecycle are well served, and which remain difficult?
- How do projects distribute control over infrastructure, data, funding, and decisions?
- Which technical standards and tools enable interoperability?
- What needs are reported by researchers and contributors themselves?
- Where could Common Knowledge add value without duplicating or undermining existing work?

Do not define the field only around technologies that are familiar or exciting. Include institutions, communities, practices, standards, and nontechnical barriers.

## 2. Source hierarchy

Use sources according to the claim being made.

1. **Primary sources:** official documentation, source repositories, governance documents, policies, licenses, annual reports, research papers, standards, and direct statements from maintainers.
2. **Independent analysis:** peer-reviewed research, credible technical evaluations, investigative reporting, audits, and independent project reviews.
3. **Community evidence:** issue discussions, forums, conference talks, user reports, and practitioner accounts. Useful for lived experience and emerging concerns, but not automatically representative.
4. **Secondary summaries:** directories, news roundups, blog posts, and encyclopedic sources. Useful for discovery; verify important claims against stronger sources where possible.

A project's own website is usually the best source for what it says it intends to do, but not necessarily for whether the system works as claimed.

## 3. Evidence and attribution

For every substantive claim, preserve enough information for another person to check it:

- Source title and publisher or author.
- Direct URL, preferably to the exact page, document, repository, or version.
- Publication, release, or last-updated date when available.
- Date accessed or verified.
- The specific claim the source supports.
- Relevant version, commit, release, or archived copy when applicable.

Use direct quotations sparingly and identify them clearly. Summarize in original language. Do not imply a source supports more than it actually says.

For software, distinguish repository activity from a working release. Where practical, record the release or commit examined, documented capabilities, known limitations, test evidence, and whether claims were independently verified.

For organizations, distinguish legal status, stated mission, actual governance, and observed practice. If a detail cannot be confirmed, mark it **not verified** rather than guessing.

## 4. Fact, assessment, and open question

Keep these categories distinct:

- **Documented fact:** directly supported by an identified source.
- **Independent finding:** supported by evaluation, testing, or multiple independent sources; describe the method.
- **Assessment:** an interpretation made by the research team, with reasoning and confidence stated.
- **Open question:** important information that remains unknown or disputed.

Avoid presenting promotional language, roadmap promises, or self-descriptions as independently established facts.

## 5. Confidence

Use qualitative confidence labels, not false numerical precision:

- **High:** directly supported by authoritative primary evidence and, where material, independent corroboration.
- **Medium:** supported by credible evidence, but with limited corroboration, incomplete detail, or some ambiguity.
- **Low:** based on indirect, outdated, sparse, or disputed evidence.

Confidence applies to a particular claim, not to an entire organization or project. Explain why confidence is limited.

## 6. Coverage and search practice

Use a repeatable search process and record its boundaries. Search across relevant disciplines and types of work, not only exact phrases such as "open science" or "distributed research." Follow references from credible sources, inspect project documentation and repositories, and look for independent evaluations and critical discussion.

For each snapshot, document:

- Research cutoff date.
- Date the research was conducted.
- Search terms, directories, databases, and communities consulted where useful.
- Inclusion and exclusion criteria.
- Known blind spots, language limitations, and access limitations.
- Areas not yet investigated.

Absence from the library is not evidence that a project does not exist.

## 7. Project and organization assessment

Where relevant, assess these dimensions separately:

- Purpose and intended users.
- Activities and actual use cases.
- Technical design and dependencies.
- Maturity and evidence of operation.
- Openness: source, data, methods, standards, and licensing (these are different dimensions).
- Governance and decision-making.
- Funding and economic model.
- Contributor agency, attribution, and rights.
- Privacy, security, safety, and responsible data stewardship.
- Interoperability, portability, and continuity.
- Strengths, limitations, risks, and unresolved questions.
- Potential relevance to Common Knowledge.

Do not collapse these into a single score. A project may be technically mature but centralized, or openly licensed but difficult to reproduce.

## 8. Comparisons and recommendations

Compare like with like. State the criteria and evidence behind comparisons. Avoid rankings unless the method is explicit and defensible.

Recommendations for Common Knowledge should identify:
- The need being addressed.
- Who experiences it and what evidence supports that.
- Existing tools or organizations already addressing it.
- Why current approaches may be insufficient for this use case.
- Whether integration, partnership, documentation, or advocacy would be better than building.
- Risks, trade-offs, and what evidence would change the recommendation.

## 9. Snapshot integrity and updates

Each snapshot has a fixed cutoff date and should preserve the state of evidence as researched. Do not silently replace old assessments with current information.

- Correct clear factual or transcription errors with a dated note.
- Add material clarifications to the change log.
- Put events after the cutoff in the ongoing developments log or the next snapshot.
- When a source changes or disappears, preserve its title, URL, access date, and available version or archive reference.
- If an assessment changes, explain what new evidence caused the change.

## 10. Ethics and responsible research

Do not collect or publish personal information that is not necessary. Respect terms, licenses, consent, privacy, security, and community protocols. Do not expose sensitive datasets, human-subject information, security vulnerabilities, or locations of vulnerable people or species. Seek appropriate expert guidance when research involves human participants, sensitive communities, or regulated data.

## 11. Limitations

The library will never be perfectly comprehensive or permanently current. Search engines, public repositories, and available documentation are incomplete; projects change quickly; public statements may not reflect practice; and language and access barriers can skew coverage.

The appropriate response to these limits is transparent scope, source traceability, independent review, and regular revision—not claims of completeness.
