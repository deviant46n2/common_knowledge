# Workstream G — Stakeholder Conversation Guide

- **Snapshot:** November 2026
- **Status:** Exploratory guide; adapt to each participant
- **Purpose:** Understand real research and knowledge-sharing needs before proposing solutions

## Before the conversation

Explain:
- Common Knowledge is an early public-benefit project investigating how research and shared knowledge infrastructure could improve.
- The project is still researching the problem; no platform or governance model is promised.
- The purpose is to learn about the participant's work, constraints, existing tools, and unmet needs.
- Participation is voluntary. They may skip any question or stop at any time.
- Ask before recording, quoting, or attributing comments.
- Ask whether notes may be retained and whether they may be quoted anonymously.
- Do not request confidential, unpublished, personal, or sensitive data.

If the conversation is part of systematic research intended for publication or generalizable conclusions, determine whether ethics review or other formal requirements apply before collecting data.

## Conversation prompts

Do not ask every question. Follow the participant's experience and use the prompts that are relevant.

### 1. Role and context
- What kind of work do you do?
- What does a typical research or knowledge-creation project look like for you?
- Who do you collaborate with?
- What resources or institutions do you rely on?

### 2. Current workflow
- How do you find and evaluate existing research?
- How do you manage data, code, methods, notes, and results?
- How do you collaborate with people outside your immediate team?
- How do you share work and make it reusable?
- What happens to your materials after a project ends?

### 3. Friction and unmet needs
- What parts of your work consume the most time or effort?
- Where do tools or institutional processes get in the way?
- What do you repeatedly have to do manually?
- What is difficult to access, afford, preserve, or verify?
- Which problems have you already tried to solve, and what happened?

### 4. Incentives and recognition
- Which contributions are recognized in your field?
- Which contributions are undervalued or invisible?
- How do funding, hiring, promotion, and publication pressures affect your choices?
- What support would make good research practices easier to sustain?

### 5. Trust, rights, and risk
- What would make you comfortable sharing data, code, methods, or results?
- What should not be shared publicly, and why?
- Who should decide how sensitive or community-held knowledge is used?
- Have you experienced misuse, misattribution, loss of priority, or unexpected restrictions?
- What would make a new platform feel trustworthy or untrustworthy?

### 6. Collaboration and infrastructure
- Which existing tools do you wish worked better together?
- What should remain under your control?
- Would distributed compute, shared storage, or AI assistance help your work? Under what conditions?
- What would make you unwilling to use a shared infrastructure service?

### 7. Priorities and validation
- If one problem could be made meaningfully easier, which would matter most?
- What would a useful improvement look like in practice?
- How would you know it worked?
- What existing project or service should we study or support instead of rebuilding?
- Who else should we speak with, especially people whose needs are often overlooked?

## After the conversation

Record:
- Participant role and broad context, only with permission.
- Date and format.
- Topics discussed.
- Direct quotes only if specifically approved.
- Needs and constraints described by the participant.
- Existing tools or workarounds.
- Contradictions and unresolved questions.
- Potential implications for Common Knowledge.
- Whether the notes may be retained, quoted, or shared.

Separate:
- **Participant statement:** what the person explicitly said.
- **Researcher interpretation:** what you infer from it.
- **Open question:** what remains unknown.
- **Potential response:** an idea to investigate, not a promise.

## Avoid

- Leading questions such as “Wouldn't a decentralized research platform solve this?”
- Asking participants to endorse Common Knowledge.
- Treating one person's experience as representative.
- Assuming researchers want all data to be open.
- Assuming AI or distributed compute is useful before understanding the task.
- Promising funding, jobs, features, or influence in exchange for feedback.
- Collecting sensitive or confidential information without a clear need and proper safeguards.

## Suggested closing

“Thank you. This is exploratory, and we are trying to understand the problems before deciding what to build. Is there anything important I did not ask, or anyone else whose perspective we should hear?”
