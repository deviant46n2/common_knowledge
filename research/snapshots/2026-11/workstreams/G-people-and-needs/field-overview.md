# Workstream G — Initial Field Overview: People, Needs, and the Research Ecosystem

- **Snapshot:** November 2026
- **Research cutoff:** 26 November 2026
- **Status:** Preliminary orientation; no direct stakeholder interviews have yet been conducted for Common Knowledge
- **Workstream:** [G — People, needs, and the research ecosystem](README.md)

## 1. Central question

What do people who create, support, use, steward, fund, and are affected by research actually need—and where do existing institutions, tools, and practices fail to meet those needs?

This workstream exists to keep Common Knowledge grounded in lived experience rather than designing infrastructure from assumptions. It must include people outside well-resourced universities and established research institutions.

The goal is not to ask people whether they like the idea of Common Knowledge. It is to understand their work, constraints, incentives, existing tools, unmet needs, risks, and the support they already rely on.

## 2. Stakeholder groups

### Research creators
- University and independent researchers.
- Early-career researchers, graduate students, and postdoctoral researchers.
- Research software engineers, data scientists, statisticians, and technicians.
- Laboratory, field, clinical, and community-based researchers.
- Qualitative, humanities, social-science, and theoretical researchers.

### Research support and infrastructure
- Librarians, archivists, repository managers, and data stewards.
- Research IT and high-performance computing teams.
- Research software and workflow platform maintainers.
- Editors, publishers, peer reviewers, and research-integrity staff.
- Research funders and grant administrators.

### Knowledge contributors and users
- Citizen scientists and volunteers.
- Educators, students, journalists, and public-interest analysts.
- Local communities, Indigenous Peoples, and knowledge-holding organizations.
- Research participants, patients, and data subjects.
- People who rely on research but rarely influence its priorities.

### Institutional and ecosystem actors
- Universities, research institutes, libraries, museums, and public agencies.
- Open-source and open-science communities.
- Scientific societies and professional associations.
- Nonprofit and cooperative organizations.
- Commercial infrastructure providers and platform operators.

These categories overlap. A person may be a researcher, software maintainer, citizen scientist, and community member at once.

## 3. What existing evidence suggests

The initial survey literature repeatedly points to a gap between support for open research and the ability to practice it.

### Time, money, training, and infrastructure

A 2026 multidisciplinary survey of 133 completed responses (mostly Europe, recruited through open-science channels) found that lack of time and funding were prominent barriers to sharing data and code. Respondents ranked journal requirements and institutional incentives among the strongest supports. The authors explicitly warn that volunteer sampling and self-selection prevent generalization to the wider research community.

A 2024 Research Data Alliance survey of 230 participants across 37 countries found that most respondents reported institutional open-science policies, but many said their organizations lacked specific credit or rewards for open-science contributions. Data management and sharing were identified as activities deserving more explicit recognition.

### Incentives and governance, not only tools

A 2026 Open Science Monitoring Initiative scoping survey of 35 respondents from the global open-science community found that lack of incentives and recognition was rated a high or severe barrier by 74%, governance gaps by 60%, cost/licensing constraints by 57%, and metadata quality by 54%. The group favored mixed methods combining quantitative indicators with qualitative evidence and expert assessment.

This is a small expert-community survey, not a representative global researcher survey. Its value is in highlighting issues that monitoring and infrastructure efforts may overlook.

### Regional and institutional inequalities

A January 2026 preprint reporting a survey of 371 researchers in China and Nigeria found support for open science alongside lower usage, with reported barriers including time, insufficient incentives, training, guidance, and technical infrastructure. It explicitly aims to broaden evidence beyond Global North samples, but remains a preprint and its sampling methods need scrutiny.

A 2026 study of researchers in Pakistan reports concerns around lower resources, finances, bandwidth, and institutional infrastructure. These findings reinforce the need to examine access and capacity, not assume that the same tools and policies work everywhere.

### Recognition and career incentives

A 2025 UK Open Research Programme survey and local institutional surveys point to differences across career stages and disciplines. Researchers may value open research but not receive time, training, support, or career credit for doing it. Junior researchers may need mentoring and training; more senior researchers may identify workload and time as the binding constraints.

These patterns should guide questions, not become assumptions about every researcher.

## 4. What this means for research design

A needs assessment should investigate:
- The actual task people are trying to complete.
- The tools and institutions they currently use.
- Where time, money, knowledge, and access are lost.
- Which needs are discipline-specific and which recur across fields.
- Who bears the cost of open research and who receives the benefit.
- What support is available and what is missing.
- Which risks people face when sharing data, code, methods, or community knowledge.
- What would make a new system useful enough to adopt.
- Which existing services should be improved or connected rather than replaced.

## 5. Preliminary hypotheses to test

1. Many barriers are organizational and economic, not simply technical.
2. Researchers need practical support and time, not only mandates and repositories.
3. Recognition systems often undervalue data, software, curation, maintenance, and community contributions.
4. The needs of early-career and independent researchers may differ from those of established principal investigators.
5. Lower-resourced institutions and regions face distinct infrastructure, funding, bandwidth, language, and publishing barriers.
6. Openness can create risks around priority, privacy, consent, misuse, and unequal benefit.
7. Infrastructure must serve technicians, librarians, research software engineers, and stewards—not only authors.
8. A platform designed without affected communities may reproduce extractive relationships.

These are hypotheses, not findings from direct Common Knowledge interviews.

## 6. Ethical and practical safeguards

- Make participation voluntary and explain the purpose of each conversation.
- Do not collect sensitive personal, employment, health, political, or confidential research information unless necessary and appropriately protected.
- Ask permission before recording or quoting.
- Allow participants to decline questions or withdraw their contribution from the notes.
- Do not promise that feedback will be implemented.
- Do not treat a small convenience sample as representative.
- Avoid recruiting only senior researchers or well-funded institutions.
- Offer asynchronous and low-bandwidth participation.
- Provide accessible, plain-language communication.
- If conversations become systematic human-subject research intended for publication or generalizable conclusions, seek appropriate ethics review and follow applicable law and institutional requirements.

## 7. Next research actions

1. Map stakeholder groups and identify missing perspectives.
2. Conduct a small set of exploratory conversations across different roles, disciplines, career stages, and resource contexts.
3. Use the [stakeholder conversation guide](interview-guide.md) as a flexible prompt, not a rigid questionnaire.
4. Record findings in anonymized, consented notes and distinguish direct quotations from synthesis.
5. Compare reported needs with existing tools and services.
6. Look for negative cases and disagreement, not just repeated themes.
7. Revise the opportunity map based on evidence.
