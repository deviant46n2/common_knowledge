# Workstream E — Documentary Heritage, Community Archives, and Long-Term Access

- **Snapshot:** November 2026
- **Research cutoff:** 26 November 2026
- **Status:** Preliminary policy and project landscape
- **Workstream:** [E — Shared knowledge and preservation](README.md)

## 1. Documentary heritage is broader than scholarly publications

UNESCO's Memory of the World Programme addresses manuscripts, archives, films, sound recordings, and other documentary heritage. The programme's current description emphasizes preservation, access, and public awareness, including the particular risks facing heritage in conflict- and disaster-affected areas.

UNESCO's 2015 Recommendation concerning the Preservation of, and Access to, Documentary Heritage Including in Digital Form treats preservation as an ongoing process. It encourages action before or at the point of digital creation, professional care, metadata and discovery, international standards, training, cooperation, and sustainable institutional capacity.

This broadens the scope of Common Knowledge beyond scientific papers and datasets. Documentary heritage can include records that are historically or culturally important even when they are not part of formal academic publishing.

## 2. Preservation and access must be coordinated, but are not identical

UNESCO's guidance recognizes that preservation and access reinforce one another, while also acknowledging that access may be limited by law, rights, privacy, security, confidentiality, or cultural sensitivities. Its Memory of the World guidelines specifically recognize Indigenous communities' ownership or custodianship and their guardianship of access.

The 2015 Recommendation states that legitimate access restrictions should not prevent memory institutions from taking preservation action. It also calls for restrictions to be clearly defined, justified, and limited in duration where appropriate, with mechanisms for appeal.

**Assessment:** A robust knowledge commons should separate the right to preserve a resource from the right to publish or expose it. Preservation custody, discovery metadata, mediated access, and public release are distinct decisions.

## 3. Community archives and Mukurtu

Mukurtu is a free and open-source digital archive and community access platform developed with Indigenous communities. Public descriptions identify granular cultural protocols, customizable cultural narrative fields, multiple community records for the same material, and localized labels and licensing.

This is significant because conventional catalogues often present a single institutional description as if it were definitive. Multiple community records can preserve different voices, interpretations, names, and relationships to an object.

Local Contexts provides complementary tools for communicating community protocols and authority in records that circulate beyond the original community system.

**Assessment:** Community archives should not be treated as merely smaller versions of national libraries. Their purposes, governance, relationships, and rights may differ. Common Knowledge should support federation and interoperability without absorbing their authority or requiring their materials to become universally open.

## 4. Preservation before and after access restrictions

A practical distinction:
- **Preservation permission:** authority to retain, back up, migrate, or otherwise protect a resource.
- **Discovery permission:** authority to expose descriptive metadata or acknowledge the resource's existence.
- **Access permission:** authority to allow specified people to consult the resource.
- **Reuse permission:** authority to reproduce, transform, publish, train models on, or commercially use the resource.

These permissions may differ. They should not be collapsed into a single public/private flag.

For some resources, even metadata can reveal sensitive information. Community and rights-holder protocols must determine what may be described publicly.

## 5. Implications for Common Knowledge

Potential design requirements:
1. Preserve community-defined access protocols and attribution through exports and federation.
2. Allow multiple descriptions and narratives to coexist with clear provenance.
3. Separate preservation, discovery, access, and reuse permissions.
4. Make access restrictions visible without disclosing protected details.
5. Avoid copying or mirroring restricted content without explicit authority.
6. Include community-controlled archives as independent stewards, not merely as content suppliers.
7. Provide withdrawal, correction, and restriction workflows that propagate to systems under the commons' control.
8. Acknowledge that downstream copies and caches may make complete withdrawal impossible; explain this before collection and design retention agreements accordingly.

These are design hypotheses for consultation, not adopted policy.

## 6. Open questions

1. How can federated systems represent a resource whose existence itself is sensitive?
2. How should preservation rights be negotiated separately from access and reuse rights?
3. What technical and contractual mechanisms can support community authority after metadata has been harvested?
4. How should the commons handle copies made by third parties outside its control?
5. What support do small community archives need for hosting, digitization, training, and long-term preservation?
6. How can community-defined narratives be linked without forcing agreement or flattening differences?
7. How should historical collections obtained under coercive or colonial conditions be described, governed, and made accessible?

Direct engagement with affected communities and memory institutions is essential before designing solutions.
