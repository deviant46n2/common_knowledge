# Workstream E — Preservation Responsibilities and Community-Controlled Knowledge

- **Snapshot:** November 2026
- **Research cutoff:** 26 November 2026
- **Status:** Preliminary analysis; requires engagement with relevant communities and preservation practitioners
- **Workstream:** [E — Shared knowledge and preservation](README.md)

## 1. Two questions that must be asked together

A knowledge commons must ask both:
1. How can knowledge remain findable, authentic, interpretable, and accessible over time?
2. Who has the authority to decide whether, how, and for whose benefit particular knowledge is collected, described, shared, or reused?

These questions cannot be separated. A technically excellent archive can still cause harm if it ignores consent, collective rights, cultural protocols, or the authority of knowledge-holding communities.

## 2. CARE: people and purpose in data governance

The Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA) explains that conventional open-data principles such as FAIR focus primarily on data characteristics and sharing, while insufficiently addressing power differences and historical contexts. The CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance were developed to complement FAIR with a people- and purpose-oriented framework.

CARE stands for:
- **Collective Benefit**
- **Authority to Control**
- **Responsibility**
- **Ethics**

The central point is not simply that data should be technically accessible. Data practices should support Indigenous Peoples' self-determination, recognize their rights and interests, and ensure that benefits and responsibilities are not captured solely by external researchers or institutions.

**Assessment:** CARE challenges any simplistic equation of “open” with “ethical.” A commons needs a rights-aware model that can support open sharing when appropriate and community-controlled access when required.

## 3. Local Contexts: expressing community protocols in digital systems

Local Contexts develops Traditional Knowledge (TK) Labels and Biocultural (BC) Labels with Indigenous communities and local organizations. Its documentation describes labels that communicate community-specific provenance, cultural protocols, permissions, and expectations for future use of cultural materials and data.

Examples include labels relating to attribution, community voice, seasonal use, culturally sensitive or sacred material, non-commercial use, community use, and outreach. Communities can customize label descriptions and languages through the Local Contexts Hub, with review by another member of the community account.

Local Contexts states that the Hub is not a data repository or content management system: it distributes notices and labels, while communities, institutions, and researchers determine what information is shared. Its FAQ also states that Indigenous Peoples retain ownership, control, and governance over their customized label suites.

**Assessment:** This is a concrete example of metadata carrying community authority and context into external repositories. It demonstrates that interoperability should preserve restrictions and relationships, not strip them away.

**Important limitation:** Labels communicate protocols and expectations; they should not be treated as a substitute for consent, legal agreements, Indigenous law, community governance, or direct relationships. Common Knowledge must not apply labels on behalf of communities or assume that a public record grants permission to reuse its contents.

## 4. OAIS: preservation as an organizational commitment

ISO 14721:2025 defines the Open Archival Information System (OAIS) reference model. Its definition makes an important point: an OAIS is not merely a server or a storage format. It is an archive system of hardware, software, information, and policy-based processes operated by an organization and staff that have accepted responsibility to preserve information and make it available to a Designated Community.

The reference model covers ingest, archival storage, data management, access, dissemination, migration, information representation, software, and exchange among archives.

**Assessment:** OAIS frames preservation as an organizational promise to a defined community, not simply redundant copies. It is useful for evaluating whether a proposed preservation service has defined responsibilities, designated users, and sustainable operations.

**Limits:** A reference model does not itself create a trustworthy archive, fund it, or certify that a particular implementation meets the model. Conformance and operational effectiveness need evidence.

## 5. CoreTrustSeal: repository trustworthiness requirements

CoreTrustSeal publishes requirements for trustworthy data repositories. Its 2026–2028 requirements are available with extended guidance and a glossary. The requirements are intended to support an objective review of repository characteristics, with all requirements mandatory and equally weighted.

**Assessment:** CoreTrustSeal offers a structured external assessment route for repositories and a useful reference when defining what evidence a trustworthy data steward should be able to provide.

**Limits:** Certification is not a universal guarantee of every dataset's scientific quality, ethical use, or future accessibility. A certification applies to a repository and scope at a point in time; its status and coverage must be checked.

## 6. Implications for Common Knowledge

Potential principles to test:
- **No automatic-publication rule:** A contribution should not become public merely because it was submitted to a commons.
- **Community authority:** Communities should determine the protocols and governance for knowledge they steward.
- **Metadata can be open while content is restricted:** Discovery records can explain the existence, provenance, and access conditions of protected material without exposing it.
- **Restrictions travel with records:** Export and federation mechanisms must preserve rights, access conditions, attribution, and community-defined labels.
- **Consent is not inferred:** Public availability, a persistent identifier, or a permissive technical API does not establish consent for every downstream use.
- **Preservation needs accountable custody:** Long-term storage requires named stewards, funding, rights, procedures, and succession plans.
- **Independent review is not a substitute for authority:** External researchers and platform operators must not override legitimate community governance simply because they can technically access data.

These are research implications, not finalized Common Knowledge policy.

## 7. Open questions requiring direct engagement

1. How do Indigenous communities and other knowledge-holding communities want their protocols represented in federated systems?
2. How can the commons recognize collective authority without forcing communities into a single legal or technical model?
3. What mechanisms support correction, withdrawal, restricted access, and controlled reuse across mirrors and downstream aggregators?
4. What obligations arise when content is already copied into independent archives?
5. How should community-defined knowledge categories and languages be represented without flattening them into external taxonomies?
6. What would meaningful benefit-sharing look like in practice?
7. What preservation obligations are affordable for small community archives and local groups?
8. How can the project avoid extracting knowledge under the banner of openness?

This workstream cannot responsibly resolve these questions from public documentation alone. It requires listening to and working with the communities concerned, on their terms.
