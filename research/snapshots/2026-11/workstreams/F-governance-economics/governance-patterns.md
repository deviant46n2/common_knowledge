# Workstream F — Governance Patterns, Representation, and Asset Protection

- **Snapshot:** November 2026
- **Research cutoff:** 26 November 2026
- **Status:** Preliminary comparative analysis; not a legal recommendation
- **Workstream:** [F — Governance, economics, and institutional design](README.md)

## 1. Legal control and community voice are different dimensions

An organization may have a public-benefit purpose while its legal control remains concentrated in a board. It may also create community advisory bodies without giving them legal decision rights. Conversely, a member-governed cooperative may be democratic for its members while excluding people who benefit from the public mission but do not work for or belong to the cooperative.

The design question is how to distribute different kinds of authority:
- Fiduciary and legal responsibility.
- Mission and charter protection.
- Strategic and budget decisions.
- Technical and research standards.
- Community representation.
- Staff and worker interests.
- Contributor and user rights.
- Donor and funder influence.

## 2. Wikimedia Foundation: a nonmember board with structured community representation

The Wikimedia Foundation's bylaws state that it has no members and that corporate powers are exercised under the direction of its Board of Trustees. They provide:
- A board of at least 9 and at most 16 trustees.
- Three-year terms and limits of up to three consecutive full terms for most trustees.
- Up to eight trustees sourced through community and affiliate nomination processes.
- Up to seven trustees selected directly by the board.
- A rule that board-selected trustees may not outnumber community- and affiliate-selected trustees.
- A board-appointed community founder trustee position that may be renewed without a term limit.
- Trustees may not be compensated for their trustee roles.
- Assets are irrevocably dedicated to charitable purposes; on dissolution, remaining assets must go to an eligible charitable entity.

An important nuance: community- and affiliate-selected candidates are nominated through a community process, but the board formally appoints them and defines the process and eligibility. This is not identical to a direct membership election with an independent electorate.

**Assessment:** This illustrates how a nonmember nonprofit can embed community representation, staggered terms, board composition rules, and an asset lock while retaining a legally responsible board. It also shows that “community-selected” can still involve board gatekeeping. Common Knowledge should study both the protections and the limits.

## 3. Mozilla: nonprofit parent and taxable operating subsidiary

Mozilla Foundation describes Mozilla Corporation as a wholly owned taxable subsidiary serving the public-benefit goals of the nonprofit parent.

**Assessment:** A hybrid structure can separate charitable mission stewardship from some commercial operations. It can also introduce complex boundaries around intellectual property, financial flows, subsidiary governance, conflicts, and accountability. This pattern should be considered only if the institution later has substantial commercial activity.

## 4. Open Source Collective: fiscal hosting and operational separation

Open Source Collective's fiscal-host documentation says an approved project can receive fund management, payment processing, tax/legal compliance, contract review, and financial oversight without forming its own legal entity. It states that projects retain autonomy over project work, while the host remains responsible for financial oversight and ensures funds support eligible open-source work.

**Assessment:** Fiscal hosting can lower the administrative burden of an early project. It is not merely a bank account: the host holds legal responsibilities, controls agreements and compliance, and may reject activities outside its mission. Fees, eligibility, termination rights, IP ownership, data access, and dispute processes need careful review.

## 5. Worker cooperatives: democratic workplace governance

The US Department of Labor describes worker cooperatives as democratically governed, commonly on a one-member/one-vote basis. Management structures vary and can include conventional executives accountable to a board.

**Assessment:** Worker ownership can distribute workplace power and prevent investor control. But it does not automatically protect the broader public mission. Workers are only one stakeholder group among researchers, volunteers, users, and communities. A cooperative may also have different incentives around employment, capital, and asset disposition than a public-benefit charity.

## 6. Governance mechanisms and trade-offs

| Mechanism | Potential benefit | Risks / limitations |
|---|---|---|
| Voting membership | Direct accountability to a defined constituency | Membership boundaries can exclude beneficiaries; turnout and expertise challenges |
| Board-selected trustees | Fiduciary continuity and skills-based recruitment | Self-perpetuation, weak external accountability, capture risk |
| Community-elected seats | Direct community voice | Electoral legitimacy, low turnout, factionalism, eligibility disputes |
| Stakeholder councils | Gives distinct groups structured input | Can become symbolic without defined decision rights |
| Reserved powers | Protects mission-critical decisions | Can make governance rigid or unclear |
| Mission / asset lock | Limits privatization and mission drift | Legal effect varies; does not prevent all practical capture |
| Term limits and staggered terms | Limits entrenchment and preserves continuity | Loss of institutional knowledge; does not prevent faction capture |
| Conflict-of-interest rules | Reduces self-dealing and related-party risk | Requires disclosure, enforcement, and independent oversight |
| Public minutes, budgets, audits | Makes decisions and resource use inspectable | Transparency does not equal participation or control |
| Worker ownership | Distributes workplace and economic power | Does not represent all public beneficiaries |
| Fiscal sponsorship | Avoids premature incorporation and admin burden | Host controls legal/financial operations; mission fit and exit rights matter |

## 7. Founder transition and mission continuity

A mission that outlives its founder needs explicit mechanisms:
- No permanent founder veto or unreviewable executive authority.
- A defined transition from founding stewardship to shared governance.
- Clear limits and term conditions for any founder-specific role.
- Independent selection and removal procedures for board members.
- Public conflict-of-interest and compensation processes.
- Protected research independence and freedom of inquiry.
- Asset and intellectual-property transfer restrictions consistent with the mission.
- Succession plans for leadership, technical infrastructure, and custodial responsibilities.
- A dissolution plan that transfers assets to aligned public-benefit institutions rather than insiders.

The founder should be able to help establish the mission and culture without becoming the institution's permanent owner.

## 8. Open questions

1. What governance design gives researchers and communities meaningful voice without compromising fiduciary duties?
2. How should community representation be selected and independently verified?
3. How can board selection avoid both self-perpetuation and factional capture?
4. What is the right balance between membership, trustee authority, and stakeholder councils?
5. What legal mechanisms can protect mission and assets across jurisdictions?
6. How can compensation be fair, transparent, and constrained by mission?
7. What are the actual costs and exit conditions of fiscal hosting?
8. Which governance arrangements have worked under pressure, not merely on paper?
