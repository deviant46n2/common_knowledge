# Workstream F — Initial Field Overview: Governance, Economics, and Institutional Design

- **Snapshot:** November 2026
- **Research cutoff:** 26 November 2026
- **Status:** Preliminary orientation; not legal or tax advice
- **Workstream:** [F — Governance, economics, and institutional design](README.md)

## 1. Central question

What legal, governance, and economic arrangements can keep a shared knowledge institution accountable to its public mission over time, while supporting paid work, broad participation, sustainable operations, and resistance to capture?

Common Knowledge's stated ambitions raise several distinct questions:
- Who owns or controls the institution and its assets?
- Who appoints and removes governors?
- Who can change the mission, bylaws, or charter?
- How are staff, contributors, members, and communities represented?
- Who controls funding and sets priorities?
- What compensation is reasonable and who determines it?
- What happens if the organization closes, merges, or changes direction?
- How can donors, governments, companies, or founders be prevented from purchasing control?

No single legal form answers all of these questions. A nonprofit can still be centralized. A cooperative can still exclude important stakeholders. A foundation can protect assets but concentrate power in trustees. Open financial reporting does not automatically create democratic control.

## 2. Distinctions that matter

- **Legal form:** The entity recognized under the law of a particular jurisdiction.
- **Tax status:** A separate legal determination that may apply to an entity if it meets statutory requirements.
- **Governance:** How decisions are made, who has authority, how leaders are selected, and how they are held accountable.
- **Ownership and control:** Who has rights over assets, intellectual property, membership, voting, and strategic direction.
- **Public benefit:** The purpose and beneficiaries an organization is legally and operationally organized to serve.
- **Member benefit:** A structure organized primarily for the mutual benefit of members, workers, or patrons.
- **Fiscal sponsorship / fiscal hosting:** An existing organization provides a legal and financial home for a project under an agreed scope, policies, and fee arrangement.
- **Foundation:** A term used for different legal forms in different jurisdictions; it does not itself determine governance or tax treatment.
- **Worker cooperative:** A member-owned and democratically governed enterprise, often using one-member/one-vote principles.
- **Stewardship:** Ongoing responsibility for mission, assets, rights, continuity, and accountability.

A nonprofit is not necessarily a charity; a charity is not necessarily member-governed; a cooperative is not necessarily a public charity; and fiscal hosting does not automatically transfer all governance rights to a hosted project.

## 3. Initial institutional patterns

### Public-benefit nonprofit with a board

The Wikimedia Foundation is a US 501(c)(3) nonprofit. Its bylaws state that it has no statutory members and that the Board of Trustees exercises corporate powers. The Foundation publishes board resolutions, annual plans, audited financial statements, and tax filings.

This demonstrates the strengths and trade-offs of a nonmember nonprofit: formal public-benefit purpose, a board with legal responsibility, and substantial transparency, but no general membership voting directly on corporate affairs.

### Nonprofit parent with a taxable subsidiary

Mozilla Foundation's description of Mozilla Corporation states that the corporation is a wholly owned taxable subsidiary established to serve the nonprofit parent's public-benefit goals.

This illustrates a hybrid structure where commercial operations can occur in a subsidiary while mission stewardship remains with a nonprofit parent. It also creates additional complexity around control, transactions, tax, intellectual property, and accountability between entities.

### Fiscal host for an unincorporated project

Open Source Collective describes itself as a US 501(c)(6) nonprofit fiscal host that provides accounting, financial administration, contracts, and other legal/operational infrastructure to open-source projects. Its public documentation describes transparent project budgets and a fee model.

This illustrates how a project can begin operating with a legal and financial home before forming its own entity. But a fiscal host has its own mission, eligibility criteria, fee structure, and oversight obligations; it is not a neutral bank account and is not necessarily suitable for every public-benefit research initiative.

### Worker cooperative

The US Department of Labor describes worker cooperatives as democratically governed, commonly using one-member/one-vote principles. Management arrangements vary: some retain a conventional executive/board structure, while others use different models.

This offers a possible route for democratic worker participation, but it does not automatically provide broad public-benefit governance or represent researchers, volunteers, donors, and communities who are not workers.

## 4. Compensation, private benefit, and mission protection

US 501(c)(3) rules prohibit private inurement and substantial private benefit. The IRS distinguishes impermissible private benefit from reasonable salaries and services delivered as part of the exempt purpose. Compensation is assessed against fair market value for comparable services under similar circumstances, and related-party transactions require careful controls.

This means that a public-benefit nonprofit can employ its founder and staff and pay reasonable compensation. It cannot operate as a mechanism for insiders to extract the organization's net earnings or assets for private gain.

These rules are relevant to Common Knowledge's economic philosophy, but they do not by themselves establish a salary cap, prevent mission drift, ensure democratic governance, or prevent donors and board members from gaining excessive practical influence.

## 5. Governance mechanisms worth comparing

1. **Membership:** Who can become a voting member, and what rights do members hold?
2. **Board composition:** How are trustees selected, rotated, removed, and held accountable?
3. **Stakeholder representation:** How are researchers, contributors, workers, users, and affected communities represented?
4. **Mission lock:** What prevents a future board or funder from changing the purpose or selling key assets?
5. **Asset lock:** What happens to assets and intellectual property if the organization dissolves?
6. **Compensation controls:** Who approves salaries and related-party transactions, and what information is public?
7. **Funding independence:** How are donor concentration, restricted grants, and conflicts of interest handled?
8. **Transparency:** What budgets, minutes, policies, conflicts, and decisions are disclosed?
9. **Appeals and dissent:** How can contributors challenge decisions and protect legitimate disagreement?
10. **Succession:** How does the institution survive founder departure, board turnover, or financial crisis?

## 6. Early implications for Common Knowledge

- Do not choose a legal form before defining governance and asset-control requirements.
- Separate the founder's role from permanent institutional authority.
- Avoid relying on founder goodwill as the primary anti-capture mechanism.
- Design compensation policies that support fair work without creating wealth extraction.
- Investigate fiscal sponsorship as a possible early-stage option, not an assumed solution.
- Distinguish legal accountability from community participation and democratic legitimacy.
- Ensure donors cannot purchase governance rights or suppress research.
- Make dissolution, succession, and transfer of assets part of the design from the beginning.

These are research priorities, not legal recommendations.

## 7. Questions for the next research pass

1. Which legal forms are available in plausible jurisdictions, and what are their governance consequences?
2. How do member-governed and nonmember nonprofits compare in accountability and mission continuity?
3. What mechanisms protect public-benefit assets from sale, privatization, or founder capture?
4. How can staff be paid fairly while preventing excessive compensation and private enrichment?
5. What is the role and cost of fiscal sponsorship at the research and infrastructure stage?
6. How can contributors and communities participate in governance without making decisions unworkable?
7. What donor-concentration limits and funding transparency rules are practical?
8. How should intellectual property, software, data, trademarks, and infrastructure be held?
9. How can the institution preserve scientific independence when major funders have strong interests in research outcomes?
10. What governance structures have succeeded or failed in comparable global public-interest infrastructure?

## 8. Scope and limitations

This first orientation is weighted toward US nonprofit and open-source institution examples. It is not a survey of international legal forms and is not legal or tax advice. Legal form, tax status, charitable solicitation, employment, intellectual property, and governance must be reviewed by qualified counsel in the relevant jurisdictions before implementation.
