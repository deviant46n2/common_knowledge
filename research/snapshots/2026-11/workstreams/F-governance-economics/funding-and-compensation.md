# Workstream F — Funding Independence, Compensation, and Financial Sustainability

- **Snapshot:** November 2026
- **Research cutoff:** 26 November 2026
- **Status:** Preliminary financial and governance analysis; not financial or legal advice
- **Workstream:** [F — Governance, economics, and institutional design](README.md)

## 1. Funding is a governance issue

Funding determines which work can be done, which risks an institution can take, which communities it can serve, and how vulnerable it is to pressure. A funding model should be assessed not only by how much money it raises but by who supplies it, what restrictions attach, how predictable it is, and whether contributors can influence governance or research outcomes.

A project may be formally independent while depending so heavily on one donor, government contract, company, or platform provider that it is practically constrained.

## 2. Funding models and their trade-offs

| Funding source | Potential strengths | Risks and design questions |
|---|---|---|
| Small individual donations | Broad participation; reduced reliance on one large donor | Fundraising costs, volatility, public attention, donor fatigue |
| Major philanthropy | Can fund long-term infrastructure and early-stage work | Donor concentration, restricted agendas, reputational leverage |
| Research grants | Supports specific public-interest research | Project restrictions, short cycles, reporting burden, funder priorities |
| Public grants/contracts | Can support durable public infrastructure | Political shifts, procurement constraints, state influence, dependence |
| Earned revenue/services | Can diversify income and align with real utility | Mission drift, unequal access, commercial pressure, conflicts |
| Membership dues | Creates recurring revenue and member accountability | Membership boundaries and voting power can privilege paying members |
| Endowment/investment income | Can provide long-term stability | Market risk, investment ethics, capital concentration, governance of reserves |
| Fiscal sponsorship | Avoids early entity formation and administrative overhead | Host fees, eligibility restrictions, legal control, exit conditions |
| In-kind contributions | Provides skills, compute, hosting, and facilities | Hidden costs, dependency on donors, unequal influence, continuity risks |

No single revenue source is inherently independent or mission-aligned. The mix, concentration, restrictions, governance, and transparency matter.

## 3. Wikimedia Foundation: diversified donors and complementary revenue

The Wikimedia Foundation's 2025–2026 financial reporting provides a concrete large-scale example:
- Total revenue was reported at $245.5 million.
- Donations (including grants and endowment contributions) were about $214.3 million.
- Investment income was about $13.7 million.
- Wikimedia Enterprise revenue was about $13.1 million.
- Other revenue and service-related income made up the remainder.

The Foundation describes most of its funding as donations from millions of people, largely small individual gifts, with additional corporate and foundation support, investment income, and commercial revenue from Wikimedia Enterprise. Its 2026 trustee-search material describes more than 18 million donations and an average gift around $10.15, although that figure is presented as an annual fundraising metric and should not be assumed to map exactly to audited donation revenue.

The Wikimedia Endowment reported net assets of approximately $208 million as of 30 June 2026. It is a separate tax-exempt organization with its own board and financial statements. The Foundation and Endowment also have documented cost-sharing and funding relationships.

**Assessment:** Broad small-donor support can reduce dependence on a single funder, while commercial and investment income provide additional sources. But the institution remains heavily donation-funded and must continually manage fundraising, donor trust, and the possibility of changing donor behavior. A large endowment can improve resilience but also creates investment and allocation governance questions.

## 4. Open Source Collective: fiscal-host fees and transparent project budgets

Open Source Collective's documentation describes a fiscal-host model where projects can receive donations, grants, and other contributions through the host. It publishes project budgets and transactions while keeping some personal details private. Its public application information lists a fee of 10% of funds raised; this should be verified against current contractual terms before relying on it.

**Assessment:** Fiscal hosting trades a portion of revenue and some operational control for legal, financial, and administrative infrastructure. For an early project, that may be far cheaper than creating and maintaining an independent entity. But the host's fees, scope, policies, eligibility, and exit arrangements must be understood.

## 5. Compensation: fair pay without private enrichment

A public-benefit institution needs skilled people and should be able to pay them fairly. The US IRS distinguishes reasonable compensation for services from private inurement or excessive private benefit.

IRS guidance describes reasonable compensation as the fair market value of services under like circumstances and counts the full package—not just salary—including benefits, bonuses, severance, deferred compensation, and other economic benefits.

For organizations subject to US intermediate-sanctions rules, the IRS describes a rebuttable presumption of reasonableness when:
1. Compensation is approved in advance by an authorized body composed of people without conflicts of interest.
2. The body obtains and relies on appropriate comparability data.
3. The decision and basis are documented contemporaneously.

This is a procedural safeguard, not a universal salary cap. It does not by itself express the organization's ethical commitment to moderate compensation or prevent excessive pay from being benchmarked against other excessive pay.

**Assessment:** Common Knowledge could investigate stronger voluntary controls than the legal minimum, such as publishing salary bands, using independent compensation review, including total compensation, requiring conflict recusals, and setting a transparent compensation philosophy. Any cap or ratio should be tested for unintended consequences, including difficulty recruiting specialized staff or creating incentives to outsource work.

## 6. Donor independence and restricted funding

Potential safeguards to examine:
- Publish all material funding sources and restrictions.
- Disclose donor concentration and the share of revenue represented by the largest funders.
- Do not grant donors governance rights or control over research outcomes.
- Separate fundraising staff from research decisions.
- Require public conflict-of-interest declarations.
- Establish a process to reject funding that compromises the mission or contributor rights.
- Avoid funder vetoes over publication, corrections, or unfavorable results.
- Maintain unrestricted reserves sufficient for orderly operations.
- Diversify revenue over time without becoming commercially dependent.
- Publish the rationale for accepting or declining material funding.

Transparency alone does not prevent undue influence. The institution needs enforceable boundaries and an independent process for applying them.

## 7. Reserves and endowments

Reserves help an institution survive revenue volatility and give it time to respond to crises. Endowments can support long-term work, but they do not eliminate operating risk.

A reserve policy should state:
- The target range, expressed in months of essential operating expenses.
- Which expenses count as essential.
- Who can authorize reserve use.
- How quickly reserves may be replenished.
- Investment risk and liquidity constraints.
- Whether funds are restricted or unrestricted.
- How endowment distributions are determined.
- What happens if the institution must shrink or wind down.

Wikimedia's annual planning and endowment materials are useful case studies, but their scale and revenue base are not directly transferable to a new institution.

## 8. Implications for Common Knowledge

Candidate economic principles:
1. No private ownership claim over the collective mission or its core assets.
2. Fair, transparent, independently reviewed compensation.
3. No donor purchase of governance or research control.
4. Transparent material funding sources and restrictions.
5. Diversification without mission drift.
6. Reserves for continuity, not speculative growth.
7. Open budgets and audited accounts as scale requires.
8. Public explanation of commercial arrangements and conflicts.
9. Explicit policies for in-kind resources and infrastructure dependencies.
10. A planned wind-down and asset transfer process.

These are hypotheses for further governance and legal work, not adopted policies.

## 9. Open questions

1. What revenue mix best protects scientific independence without making the institution financially fragile?
2. How much unrestricted funding is needed before taking on permanent staff and infrastructure?
3. Which donor concentration thresholds should trigger board review?
4. What compensation policy reflects both labor-market reality and the project's anti-extraction philosophy?
5. Should salary bands and total compensation be public?
6. What reserve target is appropriate for a small, growing, infrastructure-heavy organization?
7. How should endowment investment policy reflect the mission?
8. What are the risks of commercial service revenue or enterprise contracts?
9. What fiscal-host fee and exit terms would be acceptable for an early-stage project?
10. How can the institution preserve independence when a major funder is also a research subject?
