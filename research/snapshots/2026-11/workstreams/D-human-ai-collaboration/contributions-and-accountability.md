# Workstream D — AI Contributions, Disclosure, and Human Accountability

- **Snapshot:** November 2026
- **Research cutoff:** 26 November 2026
- **Status:** Preliminary policy comparison; journal rules are not universal
- **Workstream:** [D — Human–AI research collaboration](README.md)

## 1. Attribution is not the same as accountability

AI systems can materially contribute to research without being able to assume the responsibilities normally associated with human authorship. They cannot reliably verify every claim, consent to publication, resolve conflicts, answer for misconduct, or take legal and institutional responsibility.

A useful record should therefore distinguish:
- Human contributors and their roles.
- AI systems and tools used.
- The tasks performed by each system.
- The extent to which outputs were reviewed, modified, or rejected.
- The human or institution accountable for the final work.

A disclosure that says only “AI was used” is too vague to support meaningful scrutiny. A statement that an AI system was “an author” can obscure who is actually responsible.

## 2. CRediT: a foundation for describing human contributions

The Contributor Role Taxonomy (CRediT) defines 14 roles for describing contributions to research outputs: Conceptualization, Data curation, Formal analysis, Funding acquisition, Investigation, Methodology, Project administration, Resources, Software, Supervision, Validation, Visualization, Writing – original draft, and Writing – review & editing.

CRediT was approved as an ANSI/NISO standard in 2022 and is licensed under CC BY 4.0. The taxonomy is designed to describe contributions; it is not itself an authorship eligibility rule. CRediT guidance says the taxonomy can be used beyond formal authorship and recommends that contributors be able to review and confirm assigned roles.

**Assessment:** Common Knowledge should investigate CRediT as a base vocabulary for human contributions. It may need additional fields for AI systems, community roles, data stewardship, consent authority, and ongoing maintenance. It should not silently treat an AI system as a human contributor or author.

## 3. Journal policies: common themes, differing rules

### Springer Nature / Nature Portfolio

The current Springer Nature AI policy uses a risk-based approach across authors, reviewers, and editors. It emphasizes that human accountability is non-transferable, AI may support but must not replace scholarly judgment, disclosure is important, and confidential manuscripts or sensitive data must not be uploaded to unsecured or public AI systems.

The policy distinguishes lower-risk assistive use from interpretive or evaluative use requiring caution, and from uses that replace accountable human judgment or compromise confidentiality.

### JAMA Network

JAMA's author instructions state that authors remain responsible for all content and must review and confirm the accuracy of AI-generated material. Use of AI to assist with content creation, revision, or formatting must be reported in the acknowledgments. The instructions warn against using AI to generate or format references because of accuracy and integrity risks.

### Royal Society of Chemistry

RSC author-responsibility guidance requires disclosure when generative AI has been used in manuscript generation, translation, formatting, figures, visualization, code refinement, or references. It asks authors to describe use in the cover letter and methods, including prompts. It prohibits presenting AI-generated content as though it were original research data or results from non-machine sources.

### APA Journals

APA guidance gives task-specific disclosure examples, including use for literature searches, translation, code, analysis, visualization, manuscript drafting, and figures. It recommends preserving prompts and outputs where relevant and says authors should defer to journal-specific requirements.

**Assessment:** These examples share themes—human accountability, transparency, verification, and confidentiality—but differ in scope, thresholds, and disclosure location. Common Knowledge should not assume one publisher's policy is a universal scientific standard.

## 4. What a useful AI-use disclosure should contain

A proportionate disclosure record could include:
- System/provider and model name or identifier.
- Model version or access date where known.
- Tool configuration and relevant parameters.
- Research task and stage.
- Whether the system generated text, code, data, hypotheses, analyses, images, or recommendations.
- Whether tools, APIs, external files, or instruments were accessed.
- Whether outputs were executed, tested, or independently checked.
- Material human review, correction, and rejection.
- Any known limitations, failures, or uncertainty.
- Data privacy, licensing, and confidentiality considerations.
- A named human or institution accountable for the resulting work.

Not every spelling correction requires a full technical log. The depth of disclosure should scale with the system's influence on scientific reasoning, data, methods, results, and conclusions.

## 5. Confidentiality and data protection

Research materials can include unpublished manuscripts, personal data, confidential peer-review reports, proprietary data, sensitive community knowledge, and information covered by consent or contracts.

A researcher should not upload such material to an external AI service unless the relevant permissions, data-protection conditions, security arrangements, and institutional rules allow it. Reviewers have additional confidentiality obligations. A model's ability to process a document does not imply authorization to disclose it to that service.

## 6. Implications for Common Knowledge

Candidate principles:
1. Human accountability for research decisions remains explicit and non-transferable.
2. AI use is disclosed in proportion to its material contribution and risk.
3. Contributors are described by what they actually did, not by prestige or tool branding.
4. AI system identity and version are recorded where practical.
5. Prompts and tool traces are preserved when they materially affect results, subject to privacy and security restrictions.
6. Unpublished or sensitive materials are not sent to external services without authority.
7. AI-generated text, code, data, and analysis are not presumed correct because a human approved the final document.
8. Human reviewers can inspect the underlying workflow and artifacts when needed.
9. A contributor can correct inaccurate attribution or disclosure.
10. Journal, institutional, legal, and community requirements remain applicable.

These are research proposals, not adopted policy.

## 7. Open questions

1. How should AI systems be recorded when they materially contribute to conceptualization, analysis, or writing?
2. Should AI system names be recorded as tools, agents, workflow components, or another category?
3. How can disclosures remain useful as providers change models and service behavior?
4. Which prompts and tool traces are necessary for audit, and which should remain private?
5. How can small teams meet transparency expectations without excessive recordkeeping?
6. What contribution model recognizes human expertise, community knowledge, data stewardship, and ongoing maintenance?
7. How should authorship and responsibility work in research conducted primarily by autonomous systems?
