# Workstream C — Research Integrity, Publication Ethics, and Correction

- **Snapshot:** November 2026
- **Research cutoff:** 26 November 2026
- **Status:** Preliminary institutional and normative landscape
- **Workstream:** [C — Scientific reproducibility and research integrity](README.md)

## 1. Research integrity is broader than detecting misconduct

Research integrity is not simply a fraud-detection function. The frameworks reviewed here describe responsibilities and conditions across the research lifecycle: sound methods, accurate records, fair attribution, transparent reporting, appropriate ethics and safety, responsible data stewardship, conflicts of interest, peer review, institutional culture, and correction of the record.

The Singapore Statement on Research Integrity identifies four principles: honesty, accountability, professional courtesy and fairness, and good stewardship. Its responsibilities include accurate research records, appropriate methods, authorship and acknowledgement, fair peer review, conflict disclosure, public communication, reporting irresponsible practices, responding to allegations, and creating research environments that support integrity.

ALLEA's 2023 European Code of Conduct is a cross-disciplinary framework for research settings. ALLEA describes it as recognized by the European Commission as the reference document for research integrity in EU-funded projects, and as a model beyond that context. The 2023 revision explicitly responds to developments in open science, data management, GDPR, AI tools, social media, research culture, and inclusion.

The US National Academies' *Fostering Integrity in Research* emphasizes that integrity depends on both individual conduct and the organizations and environments in which research takes place. Its recommendations include responsible and transparent data handling, record keeping, and sharing data, models, and code to the extent possible within disciplinary, institutional, legal, and intellectual-property constraints.

**Assessment:** Common Knowledge should treat integrity as a property of research practice and its environment, not merely as a list of prohibited acts or a reputation score attached to contributors.

## 2. Different documents serve different functions

| Resource | Function | Scope and limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Singapore Statement | Broad international principles and responsibilities | Normative statement; not a detailed investigation procedure or enforceable code |
| ALLEA European Code of Conduct (2023) | Cross-disciplinary framework for good research practice and integrity | Strong reference in European and EU-funded contexts; local law and institutional rules still apply |
| National Academies, *Fostering Integrity in Research* (2017) | Evidence-informed analysis and recommendations for research culture and institutions | US National Academies report; not a universal legal code |
| COPE guidance | Publication-ethics guidance for journals, editors, and publishers | Primarily addresses publication and editorial responsibilities, not the full governance of every research project |

These resources should complement one another, not be collapsed into one policy. Common Knowledge will need to distinguish contributor responsibilities, project-level safeguards, platform moderation, publication ethics, and formal institutional investigations.

## 3. Correction is not punishment

COPE's 2025-reviewed Retraction Guidelines state that the purpose of retraction is to correct the literature and preserve its integrity, not to punish authors. The guidance addresses when to retract, what notices should contain, who should issue them, timing, inconclusive evidence, and batch retractions.

This supports several important distinctions:

- An honest error is not the same as fabrication, falsification, or plagiarism.
- A correction may be appropriate when a limited part of a work is wrong but the rest remains reliable.
- A retraction may be needed when findings are unreliable, regardless of whether intent or misconduct has been established.
- An expression of concern signals an unresolved reliability issue; it is not simply a lesser form of retraction.
- A publication-status change should be linked to the underlying notice and preserve the prior state and history.

COPE's guidance for cooperation between research institutions and journals also emphasizes coordination while recognizing that journals and institutions have different responsibilities. A publication correction is not a substitute for an institutional misconduct investigation, and an investigation should not be confused with the editorial decision about the reliability of a publication.

## 4. Implications for Common Knowledge

The following are design implications to investigate, not adopted policies.

### 4.1 Separate evidence assessment from misconduct adjudication

A platform can identify inconsistencies, preserve records, publish corrections, and refer credible concerns to appropriate independent bodies. It should not casually label a person dishonest or claim to determine misconduct from automated signals, disagreement, failed replication, or an error in a paper.

### 4.2 Provide a fair and documented process

Any future process for handling allegations should define:
- What can be reported and through which channels.
- How reports are acknowledged, triaged, and assessed.
- How conflicts of interest are disclosed and managed.
- What evidence is relevant and how it is preserved.
- How confidentiality and privacy are protected.
- How good-faith reporters and affected contributors are protected from retaliation.
- How people can respond to claims and correct factual mistakes.
- What decisions can be appealed and by whom.
- What information is published, when, and with what evidentiary basis.

These requirements need legal and specialist review before becoming formal policy.

### 4.3 Keep public correction records factual

A public record should distinguish:
- Confirmed metadata or publication-status changes.
- A publisher's correction, expression of concern, or retraction notice.
- A documented technical or analytical issue.
- An unresolved allegation.
- A disagreement over interpretation or methodology.
- An independently reproduced or replicated result.

Do not infer intent from an outcome. Do not equate a negative result, failed replication, controversial conclusion, or methodological disagreement with misconduct.

### 4.4 Protect academic freedom and good-faith criticism

Integrity processes must not become a mechanism for suppressing unpopular, politically inconvenient, or institutionally embarrassing research. Criticism should be assessed through evidence, transparent standards, and fair procedure. At the same time, freedom of inquiry does not excuse fabrication, harassment, privacy violations, unsafe conduct, or misrepresentation.

### 4.5 Do not centralize every integrity function

Different institutions have distinct authority and expertise: ethics review boards, employers, funders, journals, professional bodies, regulators, and courts. Common Knowledge should map these responsibilities and cooperate where appropriate rather than assume it can replace them. Its own role may be limited to maintaining accurate project records, clear community standards, transparent platform processes, and responsible escalation.

## 5. Open questions

1. Which principles should apply to all contributors, and which should be adapted to local law, discipline, or institution?
2. What is the appropriate role of a public research commons when a contributor or project faces a credible integrity allegation?
3. How can fair review be provided when the platform lacks a formal research institution or trained investigation staff?
4. What evidence and notice standards are needed before publicly changing a project's status?
5. How should corrections be handled for datasets, code, models, AI-generated outputs, and living knowledge graphs, not just journal articles?
6. What safeguards are needed for whistleblowers, participants, communities, and people accused in error?
7. Which external integrity organizations and procedures are suitable for referral in different jurisdictions and disciplines?
