# Founding Principles

**A working foundation for Common Knowledge**

This document describes the values that should guide the development of Common Knowledge. It is intended to help us make decisions consistently, invite meaningful criticism, and build trust with the people who may contribute to or rely on this work.

These principles are a starting point, not a finished constitution or legal governance document. They should be refined through open discussion, practical experience, and consultation with the communities affected by the project. Changes should be explained publicly, with a record of what changed and why.

## 1. Knowledge is a shared human inheritance

Knowledge is built across generations. It grows through the contributions of people working in different places, disciplines, cultures, and circumstances.

Common Knowledge exists to help make the creation, understanding, preservation, and use of knowledge a collaborative public good. Access to knowledge and the ability to contribute to it should not depend unnecessarily on wealth, institutional affiliation, geography, or status.

We should recognize prior work, respect the people and communities from whom knowledge comes, and make it easier for others to build on what has already been learned.

## 2. Freedom of inquiry

People should be able to ask questions, investigate evidence, challenge established conclusions, and explore unfamiliar ideas without pressure to reach a predetermined result.

No subject should be excluded merely because it is controversial, politically inconvenient, unpopular, or critical of a powerful institution.

Freedom of inquiry does not mean every claim deserves equal acceptance. Claims should be assessed according to the quality of their evidence, methods, reasoning, and engagement with criticism.

## 3. Evidence over authority

Expertise matters, but authority alone is not proof. Common Knowledge should encourage careful methods, independent scrutiny, and clear explanations of how conclusions were reached.

Where possible, research records should make visible:

- The question being investigated and why it matters.
- The methods, materials, and assumptions used.
- The sources and evidence supporting claims.
- Uncertainty, limitations, and plausible alternative explanations.
- Relevant funding, affiliations, and conflicts of interest.
- Negative, inconclusive, and replicated results.
- Corrections and changes made over time.

Not every project can meet the same standard or publish every detail. The standard of evidence should be appropriate to the question, and limitations should be stated honestly.

## 4. Openness by default, with justified protections

We should favor open access to research, methods, tools, and results wherever it is responsible and lawful to do so.

Openness is not an excuse to disregard privacy, informed consent, security, intellectual and cultural rights, or the safety of people and communities. Some data, locations, methods, or materials require restricted access, controlled sharing, or non-publication.

Restrictions should be proportionate, explained where possible, and no broader than necessary. Decisions should consider the rights and interests of the people and communities the information concerns, not only the convenience of researchers or the institution.

## 5. Political independence and intellectual honesty

Common Knowledge may support research into political systems, public policy, governments, parties, conflicts, economics, and other politically consequential subjects.

It must not become an instrument of any political party, government, faction, corporation, or ideological movement. The institution should not campaign for political actors, offer partisan endorsements in its name, or allow funders and powerful participants to dictate research conclusions.

Political independence is not a requirement to avoid difficult subjects or to pretend that all claims are equally supported. Findings should be communicated according to evidence and method, even when they are inconvenient to influential people or groups.

## 6. Shared stewardship and resistance to capture

The project's assets, infrastructure, reputation, and collective work should be stewarded for its public mission.

Governance should be designed to prevent undue concentration of power and influence. Funding should not buy control over research, conclusions, appointments, or institutional decisions. Conflicts of interest should be disclosed and managed.

No founder, donor, company, government, or other participant should be able to privately capture the commons or unilaterally redirect its purpose.

The exact legal and organizational structures needed to uphold this principle remain to be researched and developed with qualified advice and broad participation.

## 7. Fair work, no excessive private enrichment

People who build and maintain Common Knowledge should be treated with dignity and compensated fairly when resources permit. Sustainable work requires realistic budgets, reasonable pay, and care for the people doing it.

The project should not be designed to produce excessive private wealth for founders, executives, funders, or other insiders. Its success should be measured by the public value it creates, the trust it earns, and the durability of the commons—not by private extraction.

Compensation policies, financial reporting, and the use of surplus resources should eventually be governed by transparent rules that serve the mission.

## 8. Voluntary participation and contributor agency

Participation should be informed and voluntary. Contributors should understand what is being asked of them, how their work may be used, and what rights and responsibilities apply.

People retain agency over their own labor, equipment, accounts, personal information, and participation. No one should be expected to donate resources they cannot afford to give.

Contributions should be attributed appropriately, and the project should make room for different forms of work, including research, engineering, documentation, review, translation, maintenance, and community care.

## 9. Collaboration across people, disciplines, and AI systems

Common Knowledge should make it easier for people with different skills and backgrounds to work together. It should seek compatibility with existing tools and institutions where cooperation is useful.

AI systems may assist with discovery, analysis, translation, coordination, and other research tasks. Their outputs should not be treated as inherently authoritative. Their role, limitations, sources, and meaningful contributions should be documented well enough for people to evaluate the work.

Responsibility for consequential decisions must remain clear. Automation should support human inquiry and accountability, not obscure who made a decision or who is answerable for its effects.

## 10. Transparency, accountability, and correction

Trust is earned through conduct, not declared in a mission statement.

Common Knowledge should make its governance, funding, policies, important decisions, and use of shared resources as transparent as practicable. People affected by decisions should have meaningful ways to understand, question, and appeal them.

**Transparency begins with the founder.** While the project is founder-led, its founder's role and authority should be stated plainly. Significant decisions, relevant conflicts of interest, material commitments, project funding, and project spending should be disclosed from the beginning. The project should not claim independent oversight, audits, or accountability mechanisms that do not yet exist. The initial practical commitments are described in [Transparency](docs/TRANSPARENCY.md).

Errors should be acknowledged and corrected. Disagreements should be handled through fair, documented processes. Criticism made in good faith should be welcomed rather than punished.

Transparency itself has limits where disclosure would violate privacy, security, consent, or other legitimate protections. Those limits should not become a pretext for hiding misconduct or avoiding accountability.

## 11. Interoperability, resilience, and preservation

The commons should not depend unnecessarily on one company, platform, software stack, data center, or individual.

We should favor open standards, documented interfaces, portable data, reproducible workflows, independent copies, and systems that can be maintained or replaced by others. Where practical, important public resources should be preserved in ways that reduce the risk of loss or unilateral control.

Building everything ourselves is not the goal. Connecting, improving, and sustaining useful existing infrastructure may serve the mission better than creating another isolated platform.

## 12. Responsibility to people and the wider world

Research and technology can create benefits as well as harms. Common Knowledge should take foreseeable consequences seriously, especially where work affects vulnerable people, communities, ecosystems, or sensitive information.

We should seek appropriate ethical review, meaningful consent, responsible security practices, and ways for affected communities to participate in decisions about work that concerns them.

Scientific freedom and responsibility belong together. Neither should be used as a blanket excuse to silence legitimate inquiry or to disregard the rights of others.

## 13. A mission that outlives its founders

Common Knowledge should be built to outlast the people who begin it.

Its purpose, knowledge, infrastructure, and stewardship should be transferable. Leadership should be accountable and replaceable. No individual should hold unchecked authority or become a permanent point of failure.

The founder's role is to help establish something that others can responsibly shape, govern, improve, and carry forward—not to own the future of the commons.

## Applying these principles

These principles should guide practical decisions, not remain aspirational language.

When a decision creates tension between principles, the reasoning should be made explicit. We should identify who may be affected, what evidence is available, what rights and risks are involved, and what alternatives exist. Decisions should be proportionate, reviewable, and open to correction.

As Common Knowledge develops, these principles should inform more specific policies covering:

- Governance, representation, elections, and decision-making.
- Funding, compensation, conflicts of interest, and financial disclosure.
- Research integrity, review, reproducibility, and corrections.
- Data stewardship, privacy, consent, and access controls.
- Contributor rights, attribution, licensing, and dispute resolution.
- AI use, automation, safety, and accountability.
- Infrastructure ownership, maintenance, and continuity.

Those policies should be developed deliberately, with relevant expertise and input from the people they affect.
